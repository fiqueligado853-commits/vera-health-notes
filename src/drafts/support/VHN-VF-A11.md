A listing says “60 capsules.” The bottle beside it says “30 servings.” Both can be right. If the serving size is two capsules, those 60 capsules make 30 labeled servings. You have to find that serving size before comparing the price, the ingredient amounts, or even two photos that look like they show the same bottle.

On a U.S. dietary supplement, the **Supplement Facts** panel is where you can check what the label declares for a serving. Start there, then read the rest of the package. Keep the listing open beside it. A claim on the product page can be hard to check if the bottle photo is cropped, too small to read, or from a different version.

## Start at the top of the panel

Find **Serving Size**. The unit may be capsules, tablets, a scoop, or a stated volume of liquid. Copy the number and the unit together. A listing that describes quantity “per capsule” and a panel that describes amount “per serving” are using different denominators when a serving contains two capsules. Comparing their printed numbers as if the units matched can give you the wrong result.

Next look for **Servings Per Container**. This figure tells you how many labeled servings the package contains. The FDA’s [guide to nutrition labeling for supplements](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/dietary-supplement-labeling-guide-chapter-iv-nutrition-labeling) notes that the count can be omitted when it repeats the information in the net quantity statement. If the package details still seem incomplete, ask the seller for a readable photo of the actual item.

Here is a made-up comparison to show why the counts matter. The numbers are shopping examples.

| What the listing shows | Bottle A | Bottle B |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capsules in the bottle | 60 | 60 |
| Serving size on the label | 2 capsules | 1 capsule |
| Labeled servings in the bottle | 30 | 60 |
| Example item price before fees | $24 | $24 |
| Example price per labeled serving | $0.80 | $0.40 |

The comparison answers one narrow question: how much each labeled serving costs at those example prices. To compare specific ingredients, add a row for each amount in the panel and keep its unit attached. A serving size is a labeling quantity. It cannot tell you what amount is appropriate for you.

## Read the amounts with their units

Use the heading above the numbers to check what they apply to. If the panel gives an amount per serving, copy it that way. For example, a fictional panel showing 100 mg per two-capsule serving gives you 100 mg for the pair. A different panel showing 100 mg per one-capsule serving uses a different serving size, even though the numbers look the same.

You may see milligrams (mg), micrograms (mcg), or other units. Keep the unit in your notes. If two labels use different units, convert them before comparing. A row with a percentage can also be easy to misread when you are scanning on your phone. Check whether the number belongs to the amount column or to the percent Daily Value column.

**Percent Daily Value**, usually shown as **% DV**, expresses how much one labeled serving supplies relative to the reference Daily Value for a nutrient. Some ingredients have no established Daily Value and may carry a symbol referring to a footnote. A missing percentage in that situation does not give you a way to rank the ingredient. For an ingredient with a percentage, the figure is still a comparison to a reference value. It does not establish how well the product works or tell you how much you need.

## Find out what a proprietary blend actually discloses

A proprietary blend can appear under a name that sounds like a single ingredient. Look for the blend’s total weight, then read the ingredients listed under it. The [FDA labeling guide](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/dietary-supplement-labeling-guide-chapter-iv-nutrition-labeling) explains how ingredients in these blends are declared. In a blend with an aggregate weight, the panel does not necessarily give the weight of each constituent.

Suppose a fictional label lists “Botanical Blend — 300 mg” and identifies three ingredients beneath it. You can record 300 mg as the blend total. You cannot divide 300 by three and put 100 mg beside each name. If the quantity of a particular ingredient is important to your comparison and the seller cannot provide it, leave that cell blank. Guessing fills the spreadsheet but adds no usable information.

This also matters when one listing has a big number in its headline and a second listing has a smaller number beside an individual ingredient. First identify what each number measures. A blend total and an individual ingredient amount answer different questions.

## Read the lines outside the box

Look for **Other Ingredients**, which can include things such as the capsule material, flavorings, or ingredients used to make or stabilize the product. A panel photograph can cut off those lines. If the seller only shows the boxed area and you want the rest, request the full back or side label. Read any warnings or other statements on the package too. For a personal question about an ingredient, bringing a photo of the complete label gives a pharmacist or other clinician more context than sending a screenshot of the store headline.

Record the package size and the business name shown on the label. The [FDA’s dietary supplement Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements) describes what information supplement labels generally need to carry. The company listed may be a distributor. If you are also trying to determine who will take the payment or handle a return, check [how to identify the actual seller](/articles/how-to-tell-who-sells-a-supplement/).

If a listing shows a quality seal, check its name and, where possible, verify the exact item with the issuer. The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/WYNTK-Consumer/) explains the scope and limits of such seals. A quality seal does not establish that a product will be safe or effective for you. Likewise, the [FDA explains](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/it-really-fda-approved) that dietary supplements are not approved for safety and effectiveness before sale.

## Check that the photo belongs to the item in your cart

Check the form, bottle size, package count, and variant. A dropdown may change the price and title while leaving the gallery photos in place. If the listing offers a powder and capsules, or a single bottle and a three-pack, match the details before you use the picture to calculate anything. A photo from another market or a prior version might be a reasonable lead, but it deserves a separate note until the seller confirms that it matches what will ship.

For a quick comparison, save the full label photo, the listing name, and the date. Record the serving size, servings per container, ingredient amounts and units, any blend totals, and the other ingredients. Put open questions in their own column. You will end up with a short list of things you can verify and a clear reason to pause if the listing leaves out something that matters to you.
