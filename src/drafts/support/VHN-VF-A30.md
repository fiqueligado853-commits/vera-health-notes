You search a supplement’s name and open three tabs. Each shows the same bottle. One calls itself official, one looks like a review, and one offers a discount. Then you click through and see a fourth name on the checkout page. Which business would you contact if the item never arrived?

Start with the business identified as the seller for that specific order. Then check who handles payment, shipping, and returns. Several companies can be involved in one transaction. The same bottle photo can also appear in entirely separate listings, so carry the details from the checkout you are actually considering.

This guide is about identifying the parties to an online purchase. The example below is fictional. It does not assess any named supplement, its ingredients, or its effects.

## Follow the listing to the order summary

Open the listing you intend to use and note the website address shown in your browser. On a marketplace, look near the price or shipping details for text such as **sold by** and **ships from**. Those fields may name different companies. If the platform offers multiple sellers for the same item, switch between listings only long enough to see whether the names change. Go back to the one you actually want to examine before you compare its terms.

On a standalone store, look at the contact page, terms, and return policy. Continue as far as the order summary without entering payment details or placing an order. See whether that page names a seller, merchant, or merchant of record. If it only shows the name of a payment platform, read its terms for that transaction or ask the seller which business you are buying from. The platform’s name alone can leave the identity of the seller unresolved.

The [FTC’s online shopping guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping) suggests checking seller information, return terms, and the full cost before you pay. If you cannot reach an order summary without submitting information you prefer to keep private, stop there. A seller that can explain the purchase should be able to tell you in writing who sells the item and which policy applies.

## Match each name to what it does

Imagine you are shopping for a supplement called Example Blend. Those words are only a placeholder. A website called Example Picks writes about it. The checkout lists Sample Store as the seller. The label photo says “Distributed by Example Distribution.” The carrier sends a notification from a fulfillment warehouse. What matters is where each name appeared.

| Where you saw the name | What it can tell you | What to check next |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Review page or ad | Who published or promoted that page | Whether the publisher discloses a commercial relationship |
| Order summary near the total | Which business is shown as the seller, if disclosed | Whether that entity also appears in the applicable purchase terms |
| Label photo | Which company is listed on that package and how its role is described | Whether the photo matches the package being sold |
| Shipping notice | Who is dispatching or carrying the package | Which business handles returns and missing items |
| Receipt or payment descriptor | What name appears in the order record or billing system | Whether it matches prior disclosures and where to direct questions |

The distinction saves time if something goes wrong. For example, a warehouse may be able to locate a parcel while a separate merchant decides whether to issue a replacement. When the policy directs you to marketplace support, use the marketplace’s order page so the question is tied to the right transaction.

## Use the bottle label for the question it can answer

The [FDA’s dietary supplement Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements) describes the business information that appears on U.S. supplement labels. A label can name a manufacturer, packer, or distributor. Read the words next to the name. “Distributed by” describes a role and can be useful when comparing two packages. It does not tell you that company made the item or will sell it to you directly.

Ask for clear front and back photos if you only see a studio image. Match the bottle size, form, and variant to the selected listing. A label on a slightly different package is worth keeping in a separate tab rather than treating it as proof about the one you are considering. Our [guide to reading Supplement Facts](/articles/how-to-read-supplement-facts-label/) explains which details to capture from the package itself.

A business name can appear in several places for good reasons. Conversely, different names can be a normal part of distribution. What you are trying to establish is a written connection between the listing, the business taking responsibility for the sale, and the terms for your order. If the seller gives you a different business name from the label, ask them to explain each role.

## Treat “official” and reviews as leads

A domain with the product name or the word “official” may belong to a brand, a retailer, or someone promoting the product. An encrypted connection is important for handling personal information, but the [FTC’s shopping guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping) points out that a secure connection alone does not establish a seller’s legitimacy. Look for details you can carry from the listing into the transaction, such as a legal business name, a policy that names the same seller, and a support route tied to the order.

Reviews can tell you which questions other shoppers raised. Check the date, the exact item, and whether the review describes the same seller or platform. A review about a store that shares the product name may say little about a different store or transaction. The [FTC’s guide to evaluating online reviews](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-evaluate-online-reviews) explains why it is worth checking several sources and looking for connections between a reviewer and a seller.

A mismatch is a reason to investigate. One mismatch does not establish that a business is fraudulent. Conversely, matching logos and photographs can be copied easily. Written seller and return information linked to the exact listing gives you something concrete to assess.

## Keep the records you would need later

Before paying, save the listing URL, selected product and package, seller name, final order summary, support contact, and the policy for that order. Add the date. If the listing changes, those details can help you understand why the receipt looks different from the page you saw. Save the receipt once it arrives, and keep private information out of any screenshot you share publicly.

If a seller replies with “we handle it,” ask what “it” means. Are they responsible for shipment, refunds, or billing? Will the receipt name the same company? Which contact should a buyer use for an opened package or an unexpected charge? Get the answer from the business involved in the order, ideally through a channel the listing or marketplace documents. Then [check the refund and renewal terms](/articles/check-supplement-refund-and-subscription-terms/) before paying.

You should be able to write one sentence from your notes: “I would buy this package from this seller, and this is where I would go for help.” If you cannot fill in the sentence yet, you have a specific question to resolve before placing the order.
