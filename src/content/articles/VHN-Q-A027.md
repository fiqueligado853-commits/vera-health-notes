A cereal box shows 4% next to one nutrient and 22% next to another. Those numbers can help you compare a nutrient across boxes, but they do not give the food an overall score. Start by deciding which nutrient you are comparing and check the serving size before using the percentage.

## Ask what the percentage refers to

On the U.S. Nutrition Facts panel, percent Daily Value shows how much a labeled serving contributes to a Daily Value for that nutrient. The Daily Value is a reference, not a personalized target calculated for you. The [FDA label guide](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/how-understand-and-use-nutrition-facts-label) uses 5% DV or less as a low shorthand and 20% DV or more as a high shorthand for a nutrient in one serving. Check the nutrient name before interpreting either shorthand.

## Move the serving information beside the percent

If you regularly eat twice the labeled serving, the label’s usual per-serving estimate is half the amount you would be comparing for your bowl. Some packages already include one column per serving and one per package. Look at the heading above the percent so you do not multiply a per-package value again. If the package has no separate column, estimate the number of servings by dividing the amount you eat by the amount in one serving.

## Practice with a fictional example

Two soups show different percent Daily Values for a nutrient you want to compare. Soup A lists 10% per cup. Soup B lists 8% per half cup. If you are comparing a one-cup amount, A stays at about 10% and B is about 16%. For a precise comparison of the label’s figures, also read the quantity in milligrams or grams and compare it on the same serving basis. The printed percentages themselves can be rounded.

## Use a single comparison question

“Which option has the higher percent for fiber in the serving I will actually eat?” is easier to answer than “Which label is better?” If a second nutrient matters, record it in a separate line. A high percent for one nutrient and a low percent for another do not cancel each other out. The useful end result is a narrow comparison you can describe in one sentence.

## When a blank percentage is still useful

The label may show an amount without putting a Daily Value beside it. Copy the amount and its unit. If the package does not provide a percentage, leave that space blank in your notes and compare the amounts on a common serving basis. This keeps an absent percentage from looking like a zero. The same idea applies if a package has two columns and only one fits your question. Pick the column that describes what you intend to eat, then keep the heading in your notes.

## Leave the label with a usable note

Try writing three fields on your grocery list: nutrient, planned portion, and percent DV as adjusted to that portion. Then add anything else you care about, such as price, taste, or what would actually get eaten. If you have been given individualized nutrition targets, use the label to collect information for that plan.
