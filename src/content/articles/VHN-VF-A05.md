One VitalFlush page describes aged garlic extract and raw honey. Another lists garlic powder, hawthorn berry extract, hibiscus flower, and vitamins B12 and C. Both use the Vital Flush name for liquid drops. If you landed on these pages after seeing an ad, copying the ingredients from whichever search result you opened first could give you a list for a different bottle.

We compared the public descriptions on November 25, 2026. We could verify that the pages make different statements. We did not obtain a complete Supplement Facts photograph tied to the exact U.S. bottle sold through a particular checkout. The full formula and ingredient amounts remain unverified.

The page says it did not receive the Supplement Facts panel and does not list the amounts. |

These are commercial descriptions. [GetVitalFlush.com discloses that its operator is an affiliate](https://www.getvitalflush.com/affiliate-disclosure/), and [VitalFlushOfficial.com also discloses a possible commission](https://vitalflushofficial.com/disclaimer.html). Neither disclosure turns the page into a record from the manufacturer. The disclosures are useful when deciding how much confidence to put in what the two pages say.

There are several possible explanations for conflicting descriptions. A listing might be stale, a seller might offer a different version, or a publisher might have written a description that does not match a package. Nothing we reviewed establishes which explanation applies. We also cannot deduce ingredients that a page leaves out. A short description naming two ingredients does not establish that the label lists only two. A second page with a longer list does not establish that those extra ingredients appear in the first offer.

## A front-of-bottle picture has limits

[VitalFlushOfficial.com’s bottle and label page](https://vitalflushofficial.com/vital-flush-bottle-and-label.html) discusses details from the front of a bottle. A front photo can be helpful for comparing the name, format, and package size. You also need the readable parts of the packaging where the ingredient panel, other ingredients, and responsible business appear. A stylized product image on an ad can omit those panels entirely.

A bottle size such as 30 mL refers to the volume of the package shown or described. By itself it cannot tell you how many milligrams of an ingredient the product declares per serving. A serving labeled in milliliters is a volume. The amount of a specific ingredient, where disclosed, belongs in a different field. If you only know the bottle volume and the fact that a publisher names an ingredient, you still cannot calculate that ingredient’s quantity.

The [FDA’s dietary supplement labeling guide](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/dietary-supplement-labeling-guide-chapter-iv-nutrition-labeling) describes the serving size, ingredients, and quantities normally shown on a U.S. Supplement Facts panel. Certain proprietary blends may disclose an overall amount without giving a separate quantity for each member of the blend. If you see a blend total, keep it in your notes as a blend total. Dividing it equally across the ingredients would create numbers the label never gave you.

## What to request before comparing the products

Ask for front and back photos of the exact U.S. product on the order page you are considering. Include the name on the listing, the package size, and the selected variant if there are choices. If the seller can provide a package or lot identifier, ask whether it matches the bottle in the photos. A useful response tells you which version will ship. A stock image that appears on several different domains may leave that question open.

When you have a panel you can read, record the following.

- **Serving size and number of servings.** Copy the units and keep the volume of the entire bottle in a separate field.
- **Dietary ingredients and listed amounts.** Record any blend amount as a total. Keep ingredient names intact when comparing them. “Garlic powder” and “aged garlic extract” are different wording on these pages, and the pages do not establish that their entries refer to the same material.
- **Other Ingredients and package statements.** Ask for a full view of the packaging. Screenshots can cut off lines that do not fit inside the main panel.
- **The named business.** Copy the role alongside the name, such as manufacturer or distributor. Compare that information with the seller named for the order, since the two roles can differ.

The [FDA’s supplement Q&A](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements) describes required label information. A page should be easy to identify later, so keep the URL and date along with the response. If you are asking a pharmacist about the label, use the packaging for the exact item or state clearly when it is only an online image. This article cannot establish whether a given bottle is appropriate for someone or how it would interact with anything they take.

## What an ingredient reference can tell you

An article about garlic, honey, or another ingredient may help you understand why the name appears in an ad. It cannot settle the label comparison. Start by checking whether the reference is about the whole branded product, a particular ingredient preparation, or an ingredient in some other context. Then ask whether the reference provides the name and composition of the material studied. If those details cannot be matched to the bottle being sold, keep the reference with the ad claim it was used to support, separate from the evidence about the bottle.

That distinction matters when a result quotes a study close to the shopping link. The quote and the package image can appear side by side even when the study did not use that package or formulation. We left dose, efficacy, and ingredient-specific safety out of this comparison because the public pages do not provide the product identification needed to evaluate them.

## How to handle two labels that still differ

Keep each photo with the seller and listing from which it came. Fill in one row for each item rather than merging the information from different images. If you see matching front art and different back panels, ask the business responsible for the order to identify the version it sells. A written answer about one listing cannot establish the ingredients of a bottle from another storefront.

The [VitalFlush reviews and seller check](/articles/vital-flush-reviews/) explains what the public disclosures can tell you about those storefronts. The [price and refund comparison](/articles/vital-flush-price-refund-terms/) shows how to keep purchase terms tied to the same order. That matters here because a price from one website and a label from another can look like a complete picture when the two were never connected.

As of the date above, the verifiable answer to “What’s in VitalFlush?” is that the two public descriptions name different ingredients. One highlights aged garlic extract and raw honey, and the other names garlic powder, hawthorn, hibiscus, and two vitamins. A readable Supplement Facts panel tied to the exact bottle would move that answer beyond a comparison of websites. Until we have one, we are leaving the formula and quantities unresolved.
