The supplement video offers a “free trial,” but the checkout total only shows shipping. You can see what comes out of your account today. What you need to know before pressing the button is whether you are also agreeing to a second charge, when it would happen, and how to stop it.

Look for three answers on the same order path: what you pay today, what you may pay later, and what you must do by which date to cancel. Keep the trial terms and the order summary open next to each other. If you cannot determine the next charge or the cancellation deadline, leave the order incomplete until the seller can answer in writing.

## Find the purchase type next to the order button

Look for language such as “one-time purchase,” “subscribe and save,” “auto-delivery,” or “ships every 30 days.” Record which option the checkout actually selected. A promotion can describe one offer while the order page presents several. Check again after changing a package size or entering a discount code, because the summary is what you are using to make the decision.

A small payment today can coexist with a paid plan later. The [Federal Trade Commission’s guidance on free trials and auto-renewals](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/getting-and-out-free-trials-auto-renewals-and-negative-option-subscriptions) advises you to look for the terms of the offer, how and when to cancel, and what the business will charge after the trial. Check for links beside the order button, the price, and any consent text. Read what is included in the selected option instead of assuming “trial” and “one order” mean the same thing.

If there is a charge today, identify what it pays for. Is it shipping, a discounted first shipment, or another fee? Add the charges shown in the summary and compare the sum with the total. If you meant to make a one-time purchase, find the words that identify it before using the advertised savings as a deciding factor.

## Write down the next amount and the first date

Write down the next expected payment, the billing interval, and the cancellation cutoff. Keep those in three separate lines. A plan that bills every month, a plan that bills every 30 days, and a plan that renews on a set date do not give you the same calendar information. If the future amount excludes shipping or tax, mark that detail as unknown until you can find it in the terms.

Look for the event that starts the clock. The terms might count from order placement, shipping, or another event stated by the company. Do not start counting from delivery unless the active offer says to do that. Find out whether the cutoff is simply a date or also has a time and time zone. If the seller cannot say what controls the date, ask for a written calendar date for your example order.

Set any reminder ahead of the cutoff and give yourself time to use the required cancellation method. The reminder is there to help you act; it does not replace the official deadline. Save where the deadline came from so you can check it again if the seller’s customer portal shows a different one.

## Try the three-line check on an example

Imagine a made-up checkout that shows $5.95 due now for shipping. It separately discloses a $48 first paid shipment dated May 15, then a shipment every 30 days. It also says you need to cancel by May 12 at 5 p.m. Eastern using the account page. Those terms would put $53.95 across the first two stated charges, before any other amounts the listing separately discloses. The May 12 cutoff is the date that belongs in your reminder, even if the next shipment is three days later.

Now imagine the same offer with no price after the trial and a link that only says “manage your order.” You can still write down the $5.95 shown today. The future payment and cancellation process remain unknown. That is a useful conclusion: the first amount is clear and the longer commitment is not clear enough to compare.

These are fictional numbers. The three-line note works with a first-order discount as well. Save what is written next to the terms so you can distinguish an example from the plan you are considering.

## Check how cancellation works before you need it

Look for where the company accepts a request: account settings, a form, email, phone, or another method described in its terms. If the instructions say you will receive a confirmation, note where it should arrive. If all you can find is a general support link, ask whether that link is accepted for cancellation and whether requests are logged when submitted or when someone replies.

Canceling future renewal and requesting a refund for an order already submitted are separate actions. Ask where one ends and the other begins. An account message saying “subscription canceled” gives you useful evidence about future renewal. It may leave the status of an order already placed unanswered. Keep order numbers, dates, and confirmation messages together. When you need to identify which company handles the order, start with our [online supplement seller guide](/articles/who-sells-supplement-in-your-cart/).

If a statement later shows an unexpected charge, compare its merchant name, date, and amount with those records. Contact the seller and check [FTC advice on disputing charges](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/using-credit-cards-and-disputing-charges) for the process relevant to your payment method. The agency distinguishes different payment types, so keep the method in your records too.

## Compare an introductory offer with the one-time price

Use the case you expect to happen. If you plan to order once, write down the total for the one-time option, including the fees shown. Next to it, write the trial cost and what happens if you miss the deadline. This gives you two different comparisons: what the first order costs and what you may have agreed to spend over the next shipment. A price per bottle is useful after you know which shipments and fees it includes.

Imagine the fictional $5.95 trial above is shown next to a $39 one-time offer with shipping included. If the trial rolls into the disclosed $48 shipment, the first two trial-related charges total $53.95. That is $14.95 more than the one-time offer, though the offers may include different quantities. If you prefer a trial and can cancel under the stated terms, your comparison is different. Keep the numbers attached to the case they describe.

Write “unknown” for a future amount you cannot find. A blank cost should not become $0 on your comparison sheet. If the seller confirms the price later, update the sheet with the date and the source of that answer.

## Save the terms that belong to your order

Before buying, keep the product and option selected, the total today, the next charge, the interval, the cancellation cutoff, and the method for canceling. Save the offer as it appears in your checkout, since a headline in a video can be hard to find again. Capture the portions that show the seller and terms, with private payment information omitted from anything you share.

One useful support message reads: “Please confirm whether the item in this checkout starts auto-delivery, the amount and date of the first future charge, and how I would cancel before that charge.” A specific answer gives you a record to compare with the checkout. If the checkout and the reply disagree, pause the order while the company resolves the difference. That pause is enough to keep the small introductory price from making the rest of the decision for you.
