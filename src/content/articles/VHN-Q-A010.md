It starts innocently: coffee, a meeting, one more message, another call. Then the clock says late afternoon and you realize your longest walk was to the kitchen. If you work at a desk, movement often competes with deadlines. The easier approach is to attach activity to routines that already happen, rather than asking yourself to find a perfect uninterrupted hour.

## Attach a break to two moments you already have

Look at a typical workday and choose one morning transition and one afternoon transition. When your first call ends, you might get water. When you finish lunch, you might walk an indoor loop. Put the two cues next to your calendar so that a busy day can still remind you to move.

## Make a shorter backup

A call might run into lunch. Before joining the next meeting, change position or move around briefly if you can do so safely. Choose movements that fit your space and mobility. If pain, dizziness, or another symptom makes activity unsafe, seek individualized guidance.

## Remember the bigger picture

Short breaks support a more active day, but weekly physical activity recommendations still matter. [The CDC’s weekly activity guideline](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/adults.html) distinguishes light breaks from moderate aerobic activity and strength work. People who have been inactive can build gradually. What counts as appropriate intensity depends on the individual, especially with medical conditions or physical limitations.

## Keep what worked after five workdays

Check the two cues separately. If you moved after four morning calls but never after lunch, move the afternoon break to an event you’re more likely to notice, such as closing your last work tab. Keep the cue that already works and test the second one next week.
