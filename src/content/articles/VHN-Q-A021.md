Your cart is open, and the bottle in the product photo has a green circle that says “third-party tested.” The listing never names the testing organization. Before you decide whether to pay extra for that bottle, ask what the circle is meant to represent. Is the seller claiming that an outside lab tested a sample, or that an independent organization certifies this particular product?

If the company names a certifier, start with that organization’s own directory. Compare the entry with the item you can actually order, including the product name, form, and variant. If the company is pointing to a lab report instead, look for a lot number and the specific tests performed. Either route can answer a narrow question. Neither turns a shopping page into evidence that the product will deliver the result advertised.

## Read the words around the mark

“Third-party tested” describes who reportedly did some testing. On its own, the phrase leaves out the sample, the date, and the tests. It might refer to a finished bottle or to an ingredient that went into it. It might refer to one test or a panel. Write down the exact claim before you go looking for supporting documents. Otherwise a seller can answer a different question without making that difference obvious.

A named certification gives you something more specific to check. The [NSF explanation of supplement certification](https://www.nsf.org/consumer-resources/articles/supplement-vitamin-certification) describes the program’s label review and testing. Its [public dietary supplement listings](https://info.nsf.org/Certified/dietary/) give you a separate place to look for the product. Read the relevant program description alongside the listing. The description explains what the program covers, while the listing helps you find which product it covers.

A logo can be misread in a tiny listing image. If the description only says “certified,” ask who issued the certification and what, exactly, was certified. A manufacturing site and a retail product are different subjects. So are a finished product and a single ingredient in that product.

## Match the item before comparing prices

Copy the exact name from the listing, then note the form, such as capsules, powder, or gummies. Write down the flavor or version and any product identifier that is visible. Look at the selected option in the order summary. A store can use the same photo while showing more than one size, flavor, or formula in a dropdown.

Search the issuer’s directory by both the company and the product name. Follow the directory result to the detail it provides. Read any restrictions or program labels on the entry. When the listing identifies a specific variant, compare it with the one in your cart. A brand match is a useful lead. It does not answer whether the seller’s other flavors, formulas, or product lines are included.

Suppose an imaginary brand sells Plain Daily Mix and Citrus Daily Mix. The directory shows Plain Daily Mix, but the sale page has Citrus selected. The name of the brand matches and the product in the cart still needs confirmation. If instead the directory identifies both variants, your next question changes: are you looking at one of those exact items or a similarly named product from a different seller?

Take a screenshot or make a note of what matched, including the date and directory address. A missing match is an unresolved claim. It might reflect a different spelling or an entry you could not find. Ask the company for the issuer’s direct listing before treating the advertised mark as verified.

## Read a lab report on its own terms

If the seller offers a certificate of analysis or another lab document, look at the header before you read the numbers. Who commissioned it? Who performed the test? What sample was submitted? Look for the product name, the lot or batch number, the test date, and the result or limit for each line you care about.

A finished-product report tied to a bottle’s lot is a different kind of document from a supplier’s ingredient sheet. If you only have a sample report from the company, record it as a sample. It may show what a report looks like. It cannot identify the lot you might receive. A company that cannot give you a pre-purchase lot number may still be able to tell you whether it makes lot-specific reports available after delivery.

Also read what the report does not cover. Imagine that a one-page report lists two measurements and both meet the limits printed on the page. The document lets you describe those two results for that sample. The blank space beneath the table does not tell you that the lab performed other tests. If the ad says “tested for contaminants,” ask which ones are included and whether the company will share the full report rather than the cover sheet.

## Ask for a link someone else can check

You can make the request short enough to paste into a support form. “I’m looking at your citrus powder, 30 servings. Which independent organization certifies this version? Please send the direct listing. If the claim refers to lab testing instead, can you identify the lab and share the test panel and the lot covered?”

A reply that names the program but links only to the store still leaves you looking for the original listing. If you receive a PDF, save it with the product identification and the date rather than relying on a thumbnail. Keep the address of the directory separate from the seller’s address. That makes it easier to repeat the check when a new flavor or formula appears.

If another person is buying the item for you, send the exact listing or product identifier you checked. “Get the certified one” is hard to act on when the store shows six near-identical bottles. If you need help making sense of the ingredients per serving, use our [Supplement Facts comparison guide](/articles/supplement-label-serving-size-traps/).

## Compare two kinds of evidence without turning them into a score

Picture two listings at the same price. One gives you a current directory entry for the exact product. The other sends a report for the lot on the bottle you already own. If your question is whether this variant appears in a certification program, the directory is the relevant document. If you want to see whether a named lab reported a particular result for a particular lot, use the lab report. Put the question at the top of your note before comparing the documents.

Now add a third listing that only shows a badge in a photo. The fact that the badge looks more official than either document tells you nothing about who issued it. Give the seller one specific question. If you cannot get an answer, keep that claim separate from the price and the ingredients when you compare the three listings.

## Keep the result narrow

Your shopping note can say “directory match for this exact variant,” “lot report supplied for these tests,” or “claim remains unverified.” These are useful outcomes even when no sale happens. The first identifies a listing you could reproduce. The second describes what a lab report covered. The third tells you what you would have to learn before putting weight on the claim.

A certification or test report can be a reason to prefer better documentation. It does not tell you who will benefit from taking the supplement or whether a health claim on the ad applies to you. A study claim calls for a separate question about the article or trial behind it. The [FTC’s health products guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance) explains why the evidence must fit the claim being advertised. Keep that distinction when comparing two otherwise similar labels.
