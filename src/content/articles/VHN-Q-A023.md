The sales page shows a bottle beside the words “made in an FDA-registered facility.” In the video you just watched, the narrator talked about government standards. It would be easy to put those two things together and hear something stronger: that the Food and Drug Administration checked and approved the bottle. The registration phrase does not tell you that.

The [FDA’s consumer guide to approval](https://www.fda.gov/consumers/consumer-updates/it-really-fda-approved) explains that the agency does not approve dietary supplements before they are sold, and that manufacturing facilities are not independently approved by the agency. A registration claim is about an establishment. A claim that a supplement delivers a particular benefit raises a separate question about evidence. When you see these ideas on the same page, write down which statement refers to which thing.

## Start with the noun after “registered”

Screenshot the whole sentence and the area around it. Did the seller describe the facility, the company, an ingredient, or the product itself? The noun matters. “Manufactured in a registered facility” identifies the type of claim the seller is making about where the item was made. It does not identify what the agency concluded about that formula. If the brand leaves out the noun and uses a badge that simply says “FDA registered,” ask for the full statement in writing.

Give the screenshot a date and save the address of the page. Add the name and form of the product. A claim on one seller’s capsule listing cannot automatically be assigned to a similarly named powder. Keep the wording rather than writing down your interpretation. When you contact the seller, those few extra words often save a round of messages.

If you have both a product listing and a bottle photo, compare them. The name of the company on the package may help you ask a better question, but it does not by itself identify the particular manufacturing facility. If it matters for your decision, ask what establishment the text is referring to and what record supports the statement.

## Keep four different ideas apart

**Registration** is a claim about an establishment’s registration status under the relevant program. The phrase on the page is the seller’s statement. Writing it down separately helps you avoid reading it as an opinion on the bottle.

**Inspection** concerns what the FDA examined at a facility and what it found at a specific point. The FDA describes its authority to inspect regulated facilities. A bare registration claim does not give you the date, scope, or results of an inspection. If a seller makes a separate inspection claim, ask for the record it is relying on.

**Independent certification** is another kind of claim. If the company gives a program name, use the program’s own explanation and directory to see what it covers and whether it applies to the item. For example, [NSF’s supplement certification explanation](https://www.nsf.org/consumer-resources/articles/supplement-vitamin-certification) describes a particular program. Those terms belong to that program. They are not FDA approval.

**Product approval** is the question many people think the badge answers. The FDA’s guide says it does not approve dietary supplements before marketing. You can read the agency’s page directly rather than relying on a merchant’s summary of it. A capsule for sale can display registration language and still leave your main questions about the product unanswered.

## Check the benefit statement separately

Turn back to what made you open the ad. Did it describe the product’s contents, how it was manufactured, or what you might experience after using it? Save the exact sentence. If the ad uses an FDA-related badge next to an outcome claim, the proximity can make the outcome feel more substantiated than the words show.

A claim about an effect needs evidence for the effect being described. That is the kind of matching the [FTC’s health-products advertising guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance) calls for. A company can provide a useful facility document and still leave the benefit statement unsupported by anything it has shown you. If it names a study, ask which product and outcome were examined. A manufacturing record answers a different question.

If a company mentions a disclaimer, read the sentence it accompanies. A disclaimer can give context about an individual claim. It is not a certificate showing that an agency evaluated the product. The [FDA’s consumer resources on dietary supplements](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements) offer a place to check the agency’s own wording.

## Use a two-column note before you buy

Imagine a store page for a fictional powder says “blended in an FDA-registered facility” beneath a row of icons. Elsewhere, the page uses a graph to imply a particular result. It never identifies who created the graph or what it measured.

In the first column of your note, copy what the page says. You might write “facility described as registered” and “chart presented as a product result.” In the second column, add what you can actually check. For the first, you may have the company’s description but no independent record. For the second, you may have an image without a source. Now the unanswered questions are visible, even if the design treats both claims as settled.

Compare this with a second hypothetical seller whose product page names the certification program, links to an entry for the exact variant, and leaves out the outcome claim. That product page gives you one specific thing to verify. What you find in that listing still does not answer whether the product is suitable for you. It simply resolves one of the questions you had about the presentation.

## Keep a checklist you can reuse across ads

Make three fields: **exact wording**, **what it refers to**, and **what makes it checkable**. Under exact wording, write what you saw without upgrading a seller’s wording to a government finding. Under what it refers to, identify the facility, the product, or a health claim. Under what makes it checkable, add a source address or write “seller statement only.” The same note works for a long sales video, a product listing, or a photo of a package.

For example, suppose a marketplace title says “FDA approved” but the seller’s answer says the factory is registered. Save both statements. The reply gives you the company’s explanation, and the two statements still say different things. Ask the seller to clarify the listing. If you cannot reconcile the difference, the FDA wording is a poor tie-breaker when deciding between the two products. Put the same standard to work for the next badge you see.

## Ask for the document you actually need

Try a message that splits the questions apart: “Your page says the powder is made in an FDA-registered facility. What establishment does this refer to, and what source should I use to check the statement? Separately, what is the source of the chart on the product page?”

Save the reply. If you receive the same badge as an attachment, you still have the seller’s wording. If the support team agrees that the phrase refers to a facility and says where to find more information, you can update that part of your note without filling in the chart’s missing source. When the store’s identity is also unclear, our [guide to identifying the seller in an online supplement order](/articles/who-sells-supplement-in-your-cart/) explains where to look.

If your question is about using a product alongside a medication or to address a health concern, send the actual label to a qualified health professional. The best use of the badge check is narrower: decide what a factual-looking phrase tells you before it influences what you buy.
