Two bottles each contain 60 capsules and cost $24. One label shows a serving size of one capsule. The other shows two. The first gives you 60 labeled servings at 40 cents each. The second gives you 30 labeled servings at 80 cents each. Nothing about that difference is visible from the two numbers on the front of the bottles.

If you have two shopping tabs open, start by reading the Supplement Facts panels side by side. You can use the same approach for powders and liquids. The aim is to compare what the labels declare and what the packages cost. A serving-size comparison does not determine the amount that is right for you.

## Trap 1: Counting capsules when you meant to count servings

Start at the top of the Supplement Facts panel and find **Serving Size**. Copy the whole unit: two capsules, one scoop, or a stated volume of liquid. Then find **Servings Per Container**. The [FDA’s guide to dietary supplement nutrition labeling](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/dietary-supplement-labeling-guide-chapter-iv-nutrition-labeling) notes that the serving count may be omitted when it repeats the net quantity statement. A photo that cuts off the top of the panel can also make a field appear to be missing.

The example below uses hypothetical prices and package counts. The prices exclude shipping and tax.

| What to compare | Bottle A | Bottle B |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Capsules | 60 | 60 |
| Serving size | 1 capsule | 2 capsules |
| Labeled servings | 60 | 30 |
| Package price | $24 | $24 |
| Price per labeled serving | $0.40 | $0.80 |

If a listing offers a multi-pack, use the total number of labeled servings in the selected package and the price for that same package. If you include shipping for one seller, include it for the other when you have both totals. The calculation tells you what a labeled serving costs. Two differently formulated servings may have little in common beyond the word on the label.

## Trap 2: Comparing different units as though they match

Look at the header above the amounts. An amount listed per serving applies to the serving described at the top. In a two-capsule serving, a hypothetical entry of 100 mg per serving refers to the two capsules together. A different product that also lists 100 mg per serving may use one capsule. Start by writing the number, the unit, and what the unit refers to in the same row. If you later change the basis of comparison, your original notes still show what the label actually said.

Keep milligrams and micrograms straight. For example, 1,000 micrograms is one milligram. That makes it easier to spot when two printed numbers reflect different units rather than a meaningful difference in the amount. But matching units only gets you partway. Check that the ingredient name and the wording that identifies its form match too. If one entry is the weight of a blend and the other is the amount of one ingredient within a blend, there is no useful one-to-one comparison.

The percent Daily Value column can look like another amount column on a mobile screenshot. The [FDA’s labeling guide](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/dietary-supplement-labeling-guide-chapter-iv-nutrition-labeling) explains how these percentages are listed. The percentage is relative to a reference Daily Value. It is not a grade for quality or the percent of an ingredient your body absorbs. Some dietary ingredients have no established Daily Value and may have a footnote instead. Read the footnote before you decide that something is missing.

## Trap 3: Dividing up a proprietary blend

Imagine a panel that lists a 300 mg blend and names three ingredients underneath. You know the label declares 300 mg for the blend as a whole. You do not know that each ingredient contributes 100 mg. If the individual amounts are missing, leave those cells blank on your comparison. Sorting ingredients by name or dividing the total by the number of entries fills in numbers the label never supplied.

That matters when one store page leads with the blend weight and another highlights the amount of an individual ingredient. You can compare the information each seller makes available without turning the two numbers into equivalents. If you need an ingredient’s individual amount, ask the seller whether there is a readable label or other documentation for that exact product. Keep the answer with the listing it describes.

## Trap 4: Checking only what fits inside the box

Scroll past the Supplement Facts panel and look for **Other Ingredients**. These lines can identify ingredients such as capsule materials and flavorings. The [FDA’s questions and answers on dietary supplements](https://www.fda.gov/food/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements/questions-and-answers-dietary-supplements) describes the information that appears on the package, including details about the manufacturer, packer, or distributor. Ask for the full back or side label if the main image stops at the bottom edge of the panel.

Use that wider view to check the package size, selected variant, and company listed on the label. Changing a dropdown from a 30-count package to a 60-count package may update the price without changing a gallery image. Match the form too. A powder photo cannot establish the quantities on a capsule label. Our [guide to identifying the seller](/articles/who-sells-supplement-in-your-cart/) shows how to keep the photo tied to the listing and the business you would actually buy from.

## Try it with two listings that disagree

Suppose the title on one listing says “200 mg” and its photo shows 100 mg per serving. Before doing any arithmetic, write down whether each number names the same ingredient and which serving or package it describes. Check whether the photo belongs to the same item number and size. If you cannot reconcile the title and picture, ask for the current panel. You can tell the seller exactly which two numbers disagree and where each appears.

Now suppose both listings show the same 60-count bottle, but one sets out a single unit for $24 and the other shows a two-pack at $40. If each bottle has 30 labeled servings, the first is 80 cents per serving and the second is about 67 cents per serving before added costs. The second will still take more money out of your pocket today. If shipping or a recurring charge is shown, put those terms on their own line. The price comparison is useful once you know precisely what it includes.

## Keep a comparison you can reread tomorrow

Make one row per package, even if you are looking at two listings that use the same front photo. Add the seller, the listing date, and the selected form and size first. Then record serving size, labeled servings, the amount and unit for the ingredient you are comparing, and anything the photo leaves open. Save the photo with that row so you can tell where the numbers came from when you look at them again.

If you are comparing labels to prepare a conversation with a healthcare professional, a photo of the whole panel is usually more useful than the number from the front of the bottle. If you already use a supplement, the [one-page medication and supplement list](/articles/medication-supplement-list-for-visit/) gives you a place to record what is actually in use and what you still need to confirm.

You can finish the shopping comparison even if one of the listings is incomplete. Mark its open questions instead of relying on the artwork or the biggest number on the page. Knowing precisely what is missing gives you something specific to ask for before you spend money.
