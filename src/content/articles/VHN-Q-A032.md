A headline names a brand you bought last week. Before assuming the whole brand is involved, open the official notice and match the identifying details to the package you have. Start with the product, size, lot code, and date as the notice specifies. A shelf photo or barcode alone may leave the lot question unanswered.

## Open the notice before checking the package

Use the [FDA’s recall and safety alert listing](https://www.fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts) or the agency listed in the announcement. Read the consumer instructions and note the notice date. Watch for wording that an earlier notice was expanded or corrected. Save the current notice address with your note, especially if the product description has more than one row. A store alert can point you to the issue, but the notice should be the basis for matching the package.

## Copy the identifiers in separate lines

Look at the main product name, then the size, the UPC if specified, the lot or batch, and the printed date. Keep any punctuation and leading zeros when making a note. Look for the package location shown in the notice or its photos. A barcode on the outer box and a code stamped by a seam may serve different roles. If the code is smudged, write “unreadable” rather than selecting the closest number you can see.

## Read across one row at a time

Imagine a fictional notice lists a 12-ounce package with lot B071 and an 18-ounce package with lot C918. Your 12-ounce package has C918 printed by its seam. The size matches one row and the lot matches the other. That is not an exact match to either example row as written. If you are unsure whether you copied the correct code or the notice describes more lots elsewhere, contact the source listed in the notice with a photo.

## Record what you can establish

Your note can say “matches the fields listed,” “different from the listed fields,” or “unresolved.” If the package matches, follow the notice’s instructions. If it is unresolved, keep the item separate while you check with the contact named in the notice. A missing receipt need not stop you from reading the package, and a receipt is not a substitute for a missing lot code.

## Use the receipt for a different question

A receipt can tell you when and where you bought a product, which may matter if the notice names a retailer or distribution period. Keep it beside the package photo. If the description on your receipt is shortened, copy the full product name from the package. Try not to stretch either document past what it can show. A receipt may help narrow the search without identifying the batch on your particular package.

## Keep the evidence together

Save the notice, a photo of the package identifiers, and the date you checked. If you need to alert someone else in the household, send the exact product information and the official instruction. A message saying “all snacks from this brand” can create confusion if the notice names a specific size or lot.
