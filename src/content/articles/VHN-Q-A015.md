Two boxes of soup sit side by side. One says “reduced sodium,” the other emphasizes “natural flavors,” and both look reassuring. Which should you buy? The front of a package is designed to catch your eye. The Nutrition Facts panel helps you make a fairer comparison. You do not need a nutrition degree to use it—just a consistent sequence of questions.

## Step 1: Find the serving size first

Before comparing sodium numbers, make sure the serving sizes are similar. A product listing a smaller serving may appear lower in sodium even if the amount for the portion you normally eat is not. Pay attention to how many servings are in the package, particularly for soups, sauces, and snack foods. **In the store:** hold the two panels side by side and check the top line before comparing anything else.

## Step 2: Look at sodium in milligrams

The sodium line shows an amount per serving, which makes it possible to compare similar products. A lower number may be useful when all else is comparable, but it is not the whole story: think about taste, portions, and your overall eating pattern. Avoid choosing from the front label alone. The FDA notes that packaged and prepared foods contribute much of Americans' dietary sodium.

## Step 3: Use Percent Daily Value as a shortcut

According to the FDA, 5% Daily Value or less per serving is generally considered low for sodium, while 20% or more is high. Those numbers offer a quick orientation, not a medical prescription. If you follow a clinician-specified sodium target, your personal plan may differ. **In the store:** read %DV after confirming serving size. When the choice is close, compare milligrams as well.

## Step 4: Understand the wording on the front

[FDA labeling rules](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-D/section-101.61) set different “low sodium” limits depending on the food category and the amount used for the claim. “Reduced sodium” means at least 25% less sodium than an appropriate reference food, using the comparison basis required for that category. Check the package for the food used as the comparison. “No salt added” means no salt was added during processing, and the food may still contain sodium. To compare brands, use the milligrams for the amount you’ll eat.

## Step 5: Check how much you will actually use

A tablespoon of sauce may not resemble the amount you pour over a meal. Two slices of bread may each contribute sodium, and a can may contain multiple servings. Think about the real portion while comparing. You do not need perfect measurements at every meal; the point is to notice when a label's serving does not represent your usual use.

## Step 6: Choose a change you can repeat

An unfamiliar low-sodium alternative is not useful if you dislike it and never buy it again. Test one product in a familiar meal, then decide whether it is worth keeping. A practical grocery routine takes price, flavor, cooking time, and household needs seriously. Better label literacy is a tool for decisions, not an instruction to eliminate whole categories of food.

## One important exception

People with certain medical conditions, medications, or prescribed dietary plans may need personalized sodium targets. Follow your clinician's guidance rather than substituting general label rules. If you have questions about a specific product, a registered dietitian or qualified professional can help interpret it in context.

## Compare two cans on matching terms

Imagine a can labeled 200 mg sodium per half cup and another labeled 350 mg per cup. For an equal one-cup serving the first would provide 400 mg and the second 350 mg. These are illustrative values, not results from actual groceries. When comparing by weight instead of volume, use the grams printed beside the serving size.

## Use the same order at the shelf

The next time you compare two packages, use this order: serving size, sodium milligrams, %DV, and realistic portion. Then choose what works for your kitchen.
