Two yogurt tubs look as if they contain about the same amount of sugar. One panel starts with a three-quarter-cup serving. The other starts with half a cup. Before you judge the flavors, put the amounts on the same serving basis and read the line that says “Includes … Added Sugars.” The Total Sugars line answers a broader question.

## Put the two numbers in the right columns

The [FDA’s guide to added sugars](https://www.fda.gov/food/nutrition-facts-label/added-sugars-nutrition-facts-label) explains that Total Sugars can include naturally occurring sugars, such as those in milk or fruit, and any added sugars in the product. The indented Added Sugars line is part of that total. If the label says 12 grams total and includes 7 grams of added sugars, you record 12 and 7 in separate columns. You do not get 19 by adding the lines.

## Pick a portion before you compare

A common unit can be the weight listed in parentheses next to the serving size. Pick a portion you are likely to use. For a simple comparison, divide the label’s added sugar grams by its serving weight and multiply by your chosen weight. You can use the same approach on the total-sugars line if it is relevant to your question. Keep weights with weights. A scoop is a useful kitchen measure, but the weight of a scoop can differ between products.

## Try the calculation on two fictional tubs

In this example, Tub A lists 9 grams of added sugars per 150 grams of yogurt and Tub B lists 7 grams per 100 grams. For a 150-gram bowl, Tub A is already on the right basis and contributes 9 grams. Tub B gives 7 × 1.5, or 10.5 grams. Looking only at the printed 9 and 7 would give you the opposite order. Treat this as an estimate because the printed label uses rounded values.

## Compare the bowl you plan to eat

If you add jam, sweet granola, or another packaged topping, the yogurt label still only describes the yogurt. Look at the topping’s own label and the portion you actually add. If a topping comes in its own packet, check whether the panel describes the whole product as sold or gives a separate breakdown. A serving you measured last week may not match your breakfast today.

## What if the labels only use cups?

Check whether the labels already give weights for those cups. If only one product lists a usable weight, you can still compare using the same volume, as long as both labels actually give equivalent volume units. Write which basis you used. When a single-serve cup and a tub do not share a convenient unit, it may be clearer to write the amount for the whole single-serve cup and the amount in the bowl you normally build from the tub. That comparison answers a different shopping question and can still help if those are the two choices you are weighing.

## Save a short shopping note

Write the product name, serving weight, and added sugars at your chosen portion. Save a picture if you are comparing online listings. Recheck the actual tub when you buy it. Once you can make the comparison without guessing, you can weigh other practical choices like flavor, price, and how you use the tub.
