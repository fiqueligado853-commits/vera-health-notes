The supplement ad says “clinically tested.” You tap the little asterisk and find a sentence about an ingredient, but no citation. The most useful question at this point is simple: what did the researchers actually test? Until you can identify the study and the thing given to participants, the claim is harder to evaluate than the banner makes it look.

Start by asking for a study title, an author and year, or a direct link to the paper. Then compare the study’s product, group of participants, and measured result with the ad. This is a document check you can do without judging the medical evidence yourself. If all you can find is a study about an ingredient, record that. It is a different finding from locating a trial of the finished supplement in the listing.

## Copy the whole claim before opening the study

Save the sentence, its small print, and the page you saw it on. Where does the promise appear? A banner about the bottle, a paragraph about an ingredient, and a chart in a video can refer to different things. Write down the exact outcome implied by each one. “Clinically tested” does not tell you what the researchers set out to measure or what they found.

A statement can also have an impression beyond its literal wording. The [FTC’s Health Products Compliance Guidance](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/health-products-compliance-guidance) explains that health advertising can communicate claims through context and that the support needs to fit those claims. As a shopper, you can use that as a practical test. Would a person reasonably think the product on the screen was the product in the study? Save the material before trying to answer from memory.

Write an evidence question in one line: “Is this the same formula, and did the study measure the outcome in the headline?” That sentence is more useful than a folder full of screenshots with no labels.

## Find the original record

Follow a footnote to its destination. A seller’s blog post can point you toward a paper, but try to find the record for the paper itself. [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) lets you search by title or author. A study identifier, such as an NCT number, can help you find a record on [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/). Keep the source you actually reached, not the citation you hoped it would be.

A published paper, a registry record, and an advertisement play different roles. A registry entry can help identify what someone planned or reported. Check whether results are actually available and whether the ad links to a published report. The presence of a record is different from a favorable result or a government endorsement. If you have only an abstract, write down what it says and what you cannot see. Search by product and company as well as the advertised ingredient if you need another lead.

When a page links to a list of studies, try asking which one supports the particular sentence. A dozen papers may be harder to use than one named paper with the relevant outcome and passage identified. If the advertised result is not traceable, set it aside as an open question. A missing citation is enough reason to withhold confidence without declaring that no research exists.

## Compare the product, people, and outcome

Begin with the product. Was it the finished formulation, one ingredient, or a different formulation entirely? If the paper identifies a particular preparation, compare that name and the amount studied with the purchase listing. Some labels give a total for a blend and do not give you a separate amount for each ingredient. “Contains the same ingredient” can be true while the study-to-product match remains uncertain. Our [guide to Supplement Facts serving sizes](/articles/supplement-label-serving-size-traps/) can help you record what the label actually says.

Look next at the group and the comparison. Did researchers describe who took part and what they were compared with? Then find the outcome. If the ad talks about getting through a long afternoon, a study described in terms of a lab measurement does not establish that practical claim on its own. You do not need to interpret the lab measurement to notice that it is a different outcome.

Finally, note the duration and the result reported. Do not borrow a timeframe from the advertisement and assume it came from the paper. A citation validates the existence of a document. To support a benefit claim, the relevant evidence still needs to answer the right question. The FTC guidance gives examples of problems with transferring evidence from a different ingredient, formula, or claimed benefit.

## Work through an example without guessing

Imagine a fictional citrus powder that says “clinically tested for afternoon focus.” Its footnote leads to a study of one ingredient in a capsule. The summary describes a short task under test conditions. The ad does not identify a study of the powder, and the label lists the ingredient within a blend without stating its individual amount.

Your note might read: “Paper found. Ingredient in capsule. Task in test setting. Finished powder and advertised afternoon effect not confirmed by the source shown.” That statement preserves what you found and the questions that remain. It does not require deciding that the product is ineffective. If the company sends a second study, you can repeat the comparison and update the note rather than starting from scratch.

## Ask for the missing piece

A useful request is specific enough to answer: “Could you send the paper behind the focus statement and point me to what the participants took and what result was measured? Was the tested product the citrus powder currently sold on your site?”

Save the reply with the claim and the date. If it points to a paper, go back to that record and check the match. If it lists expert endorsements, ingredients, or testimonials without identifying a study, keep the result under “study not identified.” The answer you received might be relevant to another claim. It does not settle the one you wrote down.

## Check whether the citation has changed

Read any updates or notices attached to the original record. If you find a correction, save it alongside the paper and note which version you read. If the paper is marked as retracted, keep that label with the citation instead of copying only its original headline. If all you have is an earlier screenshot, it may miss a notice that now appears on the publisher’s page.

Sponsorship belongs in your notes too, when the record identifies it. A manufacturer funding a study and a manufacturer quoting a study of somebody else’s product leave you with different questions. In either case, record who paid and what they tested before you decide how much the citation clarifies the ad. A funding statement adds context; by itself it cannot establish whether the study’s conclusions are sound. You can leave those judgments for a qualified review and still finish the document check.

## Decide what the study can tell you

A quick screen has three outcomes: the paper matches the claim and is worth closer examination, it addresses something narrower, or you cannot trace the claim. Even the first outcome is a starting point for evaluating evidence, not a decision about taking a supplement. If the decision concerns a condition, medication, or possible side effect, bring the exact label and study to a qualified clinician or pharmacist.

This routine works on other advertisements too. Copy the promise, find the source, and check the match. It gives you something firmer to compare than two brands both using the words “clinically tested.”
