Your doctor asks how your blood pressure has been at home. You open your phone and find a few screenshots from your monitor, a number in a text message, and a note that says “higher on Tuesday.” The readings are there. Putting them in order during a short appointment is the hard part.

A paper log puts the measurements you took at the same time side by side. The date, time, and any relevant notes stay with that pair. If your monitor already stores readings or you have a reliable record on your phone, you can organize that record the same way. The format matters mainly because someone else needs to be able to follow it.

[Print the one-page home blood pressure log](/resources/blood-pressure-log/). It fits US Letter paper and includes room for two readings per session, optional pulse, and questions for your appointment. You can print a second copy when you need more rows.

## Fill in one row at a time

Use one row for one session, whether you took measurements in the morning, evening, or at a different time your healthcare professional recommended. Write the date and time first. A label like “morning” can be useful in conversation, but an actual time makes it easier to tell sessions apart when you are reading the sheet several days later. Use a.m. or p.m. consistently if your clock uses them.

Write the systolic and diastolic numbers in each reading box. Systolic is the top number; diastolic is the bottom number. If your monitor displays blood pressure and pulse on the same screen, check the labels before copying anything. Use the optional pulse space if your healthcare team asked you to track it or if you want to bring that information for discussion.

The [American Heart Association](https://www.heart.org/en/health-topics/high-blood-pressure/understanding-blood-pressure-readings/monitoring-your-blood-pressure-at-home) recommends recording two readings taken one minute apart during home monitoring. Follow the schedule and instructions your own care team gives you. If there is only one reading for a session, leave the second box blank or write “not taken.” Use a new row for the next session even if that one happens on the same day.

Here is how a single row might look. The numbers are fictional and illustrate the format.

| Date | Time | Reading 1 | Reading 2 | Session note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| May 6 | 8:10 a.m. | 132/84 | 126/81 | Interrupted during the first reading |

Write the note while you still remember what happened. Later, the phrase “interrupted during the first reading” is easier to explain than a question mark beside the number. If the session went as usual and there is nothing you want to mention, leaving the notes column blank is fine.

## Which details belong in the notes column?

Think about what you would mention if the clinician pointed to one row and asked what was happening when you took those readings. Perhaps you used a different cuff, took the measurement away from home, spoke during it, or had trouble getting the cuff to sit where you expected. A few words are enough. Save longer questions for the space at the bottom of the sheet.

A note describes the circumstances. For instance, “used a different cuff” leaves room for your healthcare team to decide whether the result needs a closer look. If you keep a log on your phone, put the note in the same entry as the readings. A separate list of notes can make it difficult to match a detail to the correct date.

Many home monitors store readings automatically. That can save time, especially when you want to show several days in order. Check whether the device clock is correct and whether more than one person uses the same memory profile. If results from two people are mixed together, bring your own written record or ask how to separate the profiles before the next set of readings.

## Prepare the log for your visit

Put the pages in date order. If your appointment is by video or phone, keep the log beside you so you can read out exact dates and pairs. If your healthcare team wants a copy in advance, ask which secure channel they use. For an in-person appointment, a paper copy can be easier to pass across a desk than scrolling through screenshots one by one.

Bring the home monitor and cuff if you have questions about how they fit or how you are using them. The AHA suggests taking your monitor to an appointment so your healthcare professional can check your technique and how its readings compare. If bringing the device is impractical, write down the model and cuff size so you have the details when you ask.

Write down one or two questions before you go. You might ask whether the team wants readings at different times of day, which readings they want you to log, or what details they would like you to add. If you missed a session, leave the gap visible. An honest record gives your clinician a clearer picture of how much information is available.

## If you already keep readings on your phone

Open the record and try to find both numbers from one session, the time it happened, and any note you made. Then check whether you can see several sessions in order without opening one image at a time. If the app or note makes that easy, you can show it directly. If the readings are scattered, you can use the printable sheet to gather the available sessions in one place before the visit.

Ask your healthcare team before calculating an average. They may want a specific group of readings or may prefer to see each value alongside any summary. Keep your original entries even if you also make a list of averages. The [CDC guide to measuring blood pressure](https://www.cdc.gov/high-blood-pressure/measure/index.html) offers additional information on taking and recording measurements.

## Put the first page to work

Write the start date at the top so the pages stay in sequence once you have more than one sheet. If you monitor more than once a day, keep each session separate and let the date repeat. This makes it easier to see where the evening session begins or to check which pair you meant when you made a note at the bottom of the sheet. If you run out of space for a longer comment, put the date and time beside it.

At the appointment, show the period your clinician asked you to track and point out one or two entries you want to discuss. If there is a series of measurements you took outside your usual routine, mention that before you review the numbers together. When you leave, add any new instructions about the recording schedule to a fresh sheet so you can refer to them the next time you measure.

## Keep the record where you need it

A completed worksheet contains personal health information. Store it where you can find it and share copies with the healthcare team as needed. The blank printable worksheet is available without an account or an email signup. It is designed for entries by hand after printing.

Use the log to support a conversation about your readings. Follow the advice you have been given about when to contact your healthcare team, and seek guidance if you are unsure what a reading means or what to do next.
