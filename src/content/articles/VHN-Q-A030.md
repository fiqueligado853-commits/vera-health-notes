You are sorting pantry items and see a date marked “Best if Used By.” That wording is a clue about quality. It cannot tell you how the item was stored or what condition it is in now. Start with the exact phrase, then handle storage and safety as separate questions.

## Copy the phrase, not just the date

USDA’s Food Safety and Inspection Service [discusses date labels on shelf-stable foods](https://ask.fsis.usda.gov/article/Is-the-dating-of-shelf-stable-foods-required-by-federal-law). The terms “best if used by,” “sell by,” and “use by” do not all do the same job. A quality date often describes when the maker expects the best flavor or quality. A store-facing date can be about inventory. There are special cases, such as infant formula, so avoid using a pantry rule as a universal rule for all products.

## Check the product and its instructions

Write down the product, whether it is opened, and any directions to refrigerate after opening. If the packaging has specific storage instructions, keep those with your note. A carton sitting unopened in a pantry and the remaining contents of an opened carton are not in the same situation. An opened product may now be governed by handling instructions that the original date alone does not describe.

## Think through two practical cases

Imagine finding an unopened package whose label says “Best if Used By” and a refrigerated item that was left on the counter with an unknown time history. The pantry label prompts you to check the product instructions and package condition. The refrigerated item raises a handling question first. The date stamp on the second item does not fill in the missing hours. Write down what you know before deciding what additional guidance you need.

## Keep recall checks separate

If there is a safety notice for the item, consult the relevant official notice and check the package details against its description. A date that looks reassuring does not supersede a matching recall instruction. When the package is damaged or handling is uncertain, look for official product-specific guidance rather than taste-testing your way to an answer.

## Use a note that can survive a handoff

If two people share a cupboard, one can place a date note where the other can see it. Include the original wording, where the food was stored, and whether the container was opened when you found it. A note that only says “expired” obscures the question. A note that says “best-quality date passed, unopened, storage directions on back” gives the next person information to check. When you cannot reconstruct storage, keep that uncertainty visible rather than editing it out of the note.

## Put date labels to work in the pantry

For unopened products you already intend to use, placing earlier quality dates toward the front makes them easier to see. Leave the original wording visible. For packages that have been opened, add the opening date to your inventory if it helps you track the instructions that apply. A date is most useful when you preserve the information about what that date actually means.
