One store tag lists cents per ounce. The next tag shows dollars per pound. The numbers can both be right and still be impossible to compare as printed. Translate them to the same kind of unit before deciding which package costs less for the amount you need.

## Read package price and unit price separately

Package price tells you the money you would pay for that item. Unit price divides the price by a standardized quantity. The [NIST Unit Pricing Guide](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/SpecialPublications/1181e2025/NIST.SP.1181e2025.html) explains why comparisons are most useful when the unit is the same. A unit of weight and a unit of volume are not interchangeable. For example, ounces by weight and fluid ounces describe different things.

## Put the tags on a common basis

For two packages sold by weight, you can compare the price per weight ounce by dividing each total price by its net weight in ounces. To convert dollars per ounce to dollars per pound, multiply by 16. If the package is sold by volume, use the same volume unit on both sides. For items sold by count, divide by the number of comparable items. Compare the same thing before trying to compare the number.

## Test a fictional shelf example

A 12-ounce bag of oats is $2.40 and a 20-ounce bag is $3.60. The arithmetic gives $0.20 an ounce for the first and $0.18 for the second. If the second tag instead shows $2.88 per pound, dividing $2.88 by 16 gets you the same $0.18 per ounce. The large bag has the lower price per ounce, while the smaller bag asks for less money today.

## Check what makes the tag apply

Some displayed prices depend on a membership or buying a minimum quantity. Read the condition before using that price in your math. For a multipack, use the total usable amount rather than just the size of each inner package. Look at the actual tag beside the exact product, since a price under the adjacent flavor can produce tidy arithmetic for the wrong item.

## Apply the same math when buying online

Online listings sometimes show a price per item for one option and per weight for another. Choose the package variant first, then find the net quantity and the total displayed for that variant. If a shipping or minimum-order charge applies to the full cart, keep it in a separate line instead of assigning it to one product at random. Save the online quantity along with the price. Then if the promotion disappears, you can update the calculation without collecting the package details again.

## Decide whether you can use the difference

For a product that you already use every week and have room to store, the bigger package may be practical. If you only need a little for one recipe, the smaller package may still be the better purchase for you. Write both the total and the unit price. One is the cost at checkout and the other lets you compare without the package size doing the thinking.
