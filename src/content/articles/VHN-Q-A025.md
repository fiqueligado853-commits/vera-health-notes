You bought a supplement after seeing a “money-back guarantee,” and now the seller’s return page talks about unopened packages. Before mailing anything, find the promise that applied to your order and ask the seller to confirm the return instructions in writing. The purchase channel, condition of the product, dates, and number of bottles can matter more than the number of days in the banner.

Start with your order confirmation. Put the offer you saw, the seller’s policy, and your description of the problem in one place. If there is a conflict, quote the specific sentences in your request. Ask where to send the item, what must be included, what deadline applies, and what amount the seller expects to refund. A clear record makes it easier to follow up whether the business accepts or denies the request.

## Identify the seller and the policy attached to the order

Open the confirmation email or purchase history and look for the order number, seller name, date, and package size. A shipping email may help you establish when the item arrived. A payment statement can help identify the charge, but its short merchant description might not be the name printed on the bottle. Our [guide to finding the seller in your supplement order](/articles/who-sells-supplement-in-your-cart/) walks through where those names can differ.

Next, locate the terms tied to the order. If you bought through a marketplace, start in the order page and follow the applicable return instructions there. If you bought directly, check the confirmation and the checkout or offer you saved. A policy on a brand’s homepage may describe a different type of purchase. A marketplace return and a manufacturer’s satisfaction guarantee may involve different contact points.

Save the page addresses and the parts that actually describe your purchase. The [FTC’s online shopping guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping) recommends finding the return policy, checking what it requires, and saving relevant purchase records. Record when you found a policy if you do not have a copy from the day you bought the product. That makes clear which document you know you had at checkout and which you located afterward.

## Turn the guarantee into a short checklist

Look for the event that starts the time period. Does the policy count from purchase, shipment, or delivery? Then find out what needs to happen before the time runs out. Filing a request and having a package arrive at a return address can be two different conditions. If the wording is unclear, ask the seller to give you both dates for your order.

Look for conditions involving open bottles, partly used packages, multipacks, and promotional items. Check whether you need a return authorization, a particular address, or original packaging. Ask who covers postage and whether original shipping or fees are part of the refund. Make a line for “amount expected” that is separate from “total I paid.” The difference is much easier to discuss when you can point to the relevant condition.

A guarantee stated in a video can be hard to locate again. If you can still open it, note when the guarantee appears and capture the wording around it. A screenshot of “60 days” is less useful when it leaves out who offers the guarantee and how the rest of the terms qualify it.

## Put a policy conflict into a concrete question

Imagine a fictional order for three bottles. You opened one and left two sealed. The offer you saved says “try it for 60 days,” but the general store policy says returns must be unopened. Your notes show that the product arrived April 8 and you contacted the seller April 22. That information gives the support team something specific to look up.

You might write: “I’m requesting return instructions for order 123. One bottle is open and two are sealed. The offer shown at checkout said ‘try it for 60 days,’ while the current return page mentions unopened items. Which terms apply to this order? Please confirm the items I should return, any authorization number, the deadline, the address, and the refund amount.”

That message does not claim a guaranteed outcome. It asks for a decision about an identifiable order. If the reply only says “send back the bottles,” ask whether it means all three or only the sealed ones. If it quotes a deadline without telling you what must happen by that date, ask whether they mean your request, your shipment, or their receipt.

## Record the return and the refund separately

When you receive instructions, save the contact date, the address supplied, and any authorization reference. If you ship the package, keep records of what went into it, the shipping receipt, and tracking if available. Save the seller’s confirmation if one arrives. If a refund is approved, look for the refund amount and the date the seller says it issued it. Compare that with what later appears on your payment statement.

If the merchant denies the request, ask which term it applied and keep the answer. If the payment itself appears incorrect or the issue remains unresolved, the [FTC’s guide on credit cards and billing disputes](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/using-credit-cards-and-disputing-charges) describes different paths and deadlines. Contact the card issuer or payment provider promptly for the process that applies to your payment method. An ordinary return disagreement and a billing error can raise different questions. The article and your records can help you describe what happened, but they do not guarantee that a payment provider will reverse a charge.

## Keep the working record easy to share

Make a single note with an order line, a policy line, and a running timeline. The order line should identify the seller, items, amount, and transaction date. The policy line can say where you found the offer and which part is unclear. On the timeline, add each contact, the seller’s response, the shipment or refund confirmation, and what you are waiting for.

Save a copy you can read without signing in to a seller account. If you need to send anything, redact full card numbers, account credentials, and other unrelated personal information. A public complaint page is a poor place to post a screenshot with your address or payment details. The seller should receive only what it needs to find the order through its support channel.

End the note with one action you want confirmed. For a request you have just sent, it may be “written instructions for sealed and open bottles.” For a refund the seller says it issued, it may be “confirm the amount and the date it was issued.” The next message can then ask for that one missing piece without reopening the whole story.

## Check future shipments as a separate task

A refund request for a shipped order tells the seller what you want done with that purchase. If you joined an auto-delivery plan, look separately for the cancellation or account-change process. Ask for a separate confirmation if you cancel. If the merchant says your refund has been approved, you can still check whether the account shows a future shipment. If the account says canceled, you can still check whether money has been returned.

Keep both confirmations if both actions apply. Put the date at the top of your file and add each new contact or shipment beneath it. When someone asks what happened, you can answer with dates, terms, and documents rather than a memory of the sales page. For a purchase you have not yet made, the same method starts earlier: save the seller’s promise before checkout, while it is in front of you.
