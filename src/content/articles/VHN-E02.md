The bottle in your cart looks just like the one on the brand's website. When you expand the delivery details, though, a store you have never heard of appears beside **Sold by**. If the wrong bottle shows up, that small line may be the first useful place to look.

For the package you have chosen, find the business named as the seller and the policy attached to that order. Then check who ships it and which company is named on the bottle. You might find the same company in all three places. You might find three different names. Either result is easier to understand when you write down where each one appears.

## Start with the item that is actually in your cart

On a marketplace, read the item page and the order summary for the exact listing you selected. Marketplace pages can show several sellers for a common product. Click the seller name if the site provides a seller profile. Check that it matches the seller shown for your selected size and form. If a different delivery date or variant leads you to choose a different listing, check again before confirming the order.

**Sold by** and **Ships from** can point to different businesses. The first may identify the party selling you the item. The second may identify who will pack or dispatch it. A platform can handle fulfillment without being the retailer for that item. The [FTC's guide to buying from an online marketplace](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/buying-online-marketplace) explains why it is worth checking whom you are dealing with and what protection the platform actually offers. Look for the rules that cover the seller and item you selected.

A standalone site may not use those exact labels. Look in the terms, order summary, returns page, and contact information for a name you can match to the transaction. If all you can find is a payment service's logo, ask which business will sell you the bottle, take responsibility for the order, and handle a problem. If the answer depends on opening a checkout page, review it without paying to the extent the site allows. You can stop if proceeding requires information you prefer not to share.

## Follow a fictional order through four screens

Imagine an ad takes you to Example Picks, a blog that links to a marketplace. The marketplace shows “Sold by Sample Store” and “Ships from Market Warehouse.” The bottle photo says “Distributed by Example Distribution.” These are fictional names for four distinct roles.

| Name in the example | What to record | What remains open |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Example Picks | Published the ad or review page you first saw | Whether it has a commercial relationship and whether it helps with orders |
| Sample Store | Listed as seller for the selected order | Whether its name matches the receipt and which return process applies |
| Market Warehouse | Listed under shipping or fulfillment | Who handles refunds if the item is delivered but wrong |
| Example Distribution | Listed on the bottle as distributor | Whether the retail seller is authorized and which bottle version it sells |

The first name in the chain may be the one you remember. The seller in the order summary is the name you want when you are checking what will happen to the money and the merchandise. When a marketplace handles questions, start with its order page, which should associate the message with the right item and seller.

## Read the wording next to the name on the package

For U.S. dietary supplements, the [FDA's labeling guide](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements-guidance-documents-regulatory-information/dietary-supplement-labeling-guide-chapter-i-general-dietary-supplement-labeling) says a label generally identifies the manufacturer, packer, or distributor and its place of business. Copy the company name and the wording that describes its role. A name under “Distributed by” may tell you something about the supply chain without establishing who made or sold that particular bottle.

Ask for readable photos when a listing only shows the front. A good response identifies the selected package size and form and tells you whether the photo is for that item. If the photo from the brand shows a different number of capsules than the photo from the marketplace, keep the two images separate until the seller explains which version will ship. Our [guide to comparing supplement labels](/articles/supplement-label-serving-size-traps/) shows where to look for the package and serving details.

If a company maintains its own list of authorized sellers, check the exact storefront name against that list or contact the brand through a channel you found independently. A familiar logo, a brand storefront banner, and a product photo can all be useful clues. None of them gives you a chain of custody for a specific bottle. A mismatch is something to ask about. It does not establish that a particular item is counterfeit.

## Check reviews against the seller and item

A review that says “never arrived” may be relevant to delivery but tell you nothing about the formula. A review dated two years ago may describe another seller, package, or return policy. When you read reviews, keep the question small. Is the writer talking about the seller I would order from, and do they say what went wrong? If you cannot tell, treat the review as a prompt for a question. The [FTC's advice on evaluating reviews](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/how-evaluate-online-reviews) recommends using multiple sources and considering possible connections between reviewers and sellers.

Do the same with claims that a site is official. A website can use the product name in its address and still be an advertising publisher. Look for a business name, a relationship disclosure, and the seller named at checkout. A padlock can tell you the connection uses encryption. It does not tell you whether the seller is trustworthy. The [FTC's online shopping guide](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/online-shopping) has more ways to check the seller and keep purchase records.

Take one more look after a link opens a new site. If the address changed, the policies from the first tab may no longer describe the place where you would pay. If you are comparing a marketplace listing with a standalone checkout, write the two addresses on separate lines. The same package image can appear in both places while the seller, support contact, shipping charge, and return instructions differ. Keep the notes tied to the exact tab and selected item.

## Get one answer you can save with the listing

If you are unsure who would handle a return, send a question that includes the selected item and size. For example, “For this exact listing, which business will appear as the seller on the receipt? If the wrong item arrives, where do I open a return and who pays the return shipping?” Ask through the marketplace or another contact route you can associate with the seller. A useful response makes clear which item and which terms it covers.

Save the listing address, date, selected package, seller, and applicable return policy before the page changes. If you buy, keep the order confirmation and billing record nearby. Redact order numbers and other personal details if you ever share a screenshot publicly. Keep the record in a place where you can find it without repeating the entire search.

When the names line up and you know whom to contact, you have resolved a practical question about the order. When one name is still missing, you also know exactly what to ask about. That is a more useful result than deciding whether to trust an entire site based on a logo or a single review.
