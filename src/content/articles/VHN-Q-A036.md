The ad lists three ingredients you recognize. The photo of the Supplement Facts panel shows one number beside a proprietary blend. If you came from the ad looking for how much of one ingredient is in the bottle, that single number is the first thing to interpret correctly. It is the total weight of the blend per serving, when the label lists the ingredients together under that blend. You generally cannot recover the individual amounts from that total and the ingredient list alone.

This guide gives you a way to record exactly what the panel says, decide what remains unknown, and ask the seller a question it can answer. The goal is to make a more informed comparison before paying. It does not tell you how much of an ingredient you should take.

## Start with the line that owns the number

Find the Supplement Facts panel for the exact product and version you are considering. Note the serving size. Then follow the number on the right back to the name on the left. Is it beside a single dietary ingredient, or beside the name of a blend with several ingredients under it? The location of the number matters more than the size of the type on the front of the bottle.

Under the [U.S. rule for supplement nutrition labeling](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-B/part-101/subpart-C/section-101.36), a proprietary blend must show its total weight, and the dietary ingredients covered by the rule are listed within the blend in descending order by weight. The [FDA labeling guide](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements-guidance-documents-regulatory-information-topic/dietary-supplement-labeling-guide-chapter-iv-nutrition-labeling) gives a separate explanation of how nutrition labeling works. A total followed by multiple names is a different disclosure from several amounts each aligned with its own ingredient.

A listing image can cut off the column that would let you associate amounts with particular lines. If the picture is blurry, do not try to infer which ingredient a number sits beside. Look for a legible picture of the full panel and confirm that it is for the same flavor, form, or quantity offered in the ad. Save that picture next to the listing link so a later update does not erase what you were comparing.

## Try it on two fictional labels

Imagine one label lists a serving of two capsules and a 600 mg blend. The blend lists Ingredient X, Ingredient Y, and Ingredient Z in that order and gives no separate amounts. A second label lists a serving of one capsule and declares 150 mg of Ingredient X directly.

Here is what you can document before you make a choice:

- **First bottle.** 600 mg of the whole blend per two-capsule serving. Ingredient X is listed within it. Its individual amount is unknown from the information shown.
- **Second bottle.** 150 mg of Ingredient X per one-capsule serving, as stated on this illustrative label.
- **Important gap.** The two labels do not let you compare the amount of Ingredient X directly. Comparing 600 mg against 150 mg would put the weight of a blend beside the declared weight of one ingredient.

Dividing 600 by the three names gives 200, but that calculation assumes equal weights. The label did not give you that assumption. Dividing the total by two might describe an average amount of the blend per capsule in this example. It still does not reveal the amount of X. Record unknown instead of giving it a convincing-looking number.

## Use the order of the names for the question it can answer

Order can tell you about relative placement under the label rule. It cannot tell you how large the gap is between adjacent ingredients. If three ingredient names appear, knowing the first one ranks ahead of the last one is different from knowing the first ingredient provides most of the blend. This distinction matters when an advertisement puts one component in the spotlight and the package shows that component somewhere inside a larger list.

Do not import amounts for individual ingredients from a different bottle, an earlier listing, or a paper about a different preparation. A manufacturer may advertise a blend name across several forms. Keep the quoted amount attached to the panel that actually states it. If you see a second number elsewhere on the package, write down what it describes before matching it to anything in the blend. A quantity can refer to a serving, the full bottle, or a separately declared ingredient.

## Turn a claim in the ad into a checkable question

Write down the ingredient or quantity the ad made you care about. A reader who wants to know whether the ingredient is named can often answer that straight from a legible panel. A reader comparing a particular quantity needs a disclosure of that ingredient’s amount. Someone checking whether an advertisement’s study applies needs additional information about the actual study and product.

If the ad links to a paper, first compare the preparation and amount in the study with the information you have. Where you only have a blend total, note the missing amount instead of assuming the study matches. Our [guide to tracing a clinically tested claim](/articles/clinically-tested-supplement-claim/) explains what else to record when reviewing an original citation. That check helps you identify gaps in a claim. It does not turn the purchase into a medical recommendation.

## What if the ad and the label use different units?

Suppose an ad promotes the amount in a bottle, while the panel declares a weight per serving. First write down both statements and their units. A bottle total and a serving total cannot be compared until you know how many labeled servings are in that bottle. Even then, converting the total leaves a blend total. It does not transform the result into an amount of the featured ingredient. If the ad never says whether its number applies to one serving or to the entire package, make that your first question. It is possible to settle that question and still have a second one about the individual ingredients.

## Ask for the exact missing amount

A short question is easier to answer than a request for “the complete formula.” You can adapt this example:

> I’m comparing the capsules in this listing. The panel shows a 600 mg blend per two capsules and lists Ingredient X inside it. What is the amount of Ingredient X per labeled serving for this exact version? If that amount is disclosed elsewhere, please send the full panel or a document that identifies the item it applies to.

Save the answer with the date, product name, and listing. Check whether the reply names the ingredient, specifies the unit, and distinguishes per serving from per capsule. If the company repeats the 600 mg total or answers for another flavor, the individual amount is still unresolved. A manufacturer may decline to provide a breakdown. That is useful information for a buyer who needs a comparison with a disclosed amount.

An answer that looks more specific is still evidence of what the company said. If another photo on the retailer’s page conflicts, keep both records and ask which applies to the bottle you would receive. When a personal health decision depends on the amount, keep the actual label and correspondence for a qualified clinician or pharmacist rather than trying to choose an amount from incomplete information.

## Leave the comparison in a form you can reuse

Keep four fields: the exact product and serving, the ingredient that led you here, the kind of amount actually declared, and the specific question still unanswered. Mark whether the source was a current label image or a written reply. Our [serving-size guide](/articles/supplement-label-serving-size-traps/) can help if two labels use different numbers of capsules per serving.

The best result may be a well-supported “unknown.” If knowing the individual amount is part of your purchase decision, that answer lets you identify which products are actually comparable and which would require a better document. The heading in the ad can tell you what the seller wants you to notice. The label can tell you what was disclosed for the item you are considering.
