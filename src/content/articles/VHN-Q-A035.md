You are making a grocery list from memory and are almost certain there is another can of beans at home. There is a quicker way to settle it than digging through every cupboard. Pick two meals you actually expect to make, look for their ingredients first, and write down only the gaps. The small inventory gives you a shopping list you can use today.

## Start with two meals, not a list of every jar

Choose one meal you know how to make and one backup you would like to have. Write the few ingredients they share near the top of the page. If neither meal is decided, use what you can see in the pantry as a starting point. The [USDA SNAP-Ed budget guidance](https://snaped.fns.usda.gov/snap/EatRightWhenMoney%27sTight.pdf) is a broader resource for fitting grocery choices to the meals and household you are planning for.

## Check the physical amount before calling it covered

An opened package can look full from the front of the shelf. Pick it up and estimate the amount in the unit you need. Write “one can,” “about half a bag,” or “not enough” next to the ingredient. Check the cabinet, refrigerator, or freezer if you sometimes keep the same food in more than one place. For opened or perishable items, keep handling and storage checks separate from the count.

## Work through a five-minute example

Suppose you want to make tomato-bean soup and a rice bowl. You find one can of beans, a full bag of rice, half a jar of tomato sauce, and no onions. Write beans on the list only if both meals need more than the can you have. Keep rice off the list. For tomato sauce, check whether the amount and the storage instructions fit the meal. Put onions beside either a purchase or a substitute you already have.

## Use three words to build the cart

For each ingredient, write **have**, **need**, or **check**. “Have” stays on the meal plan. “Need” goes on the shopping list in a useful amount. “Check” gets a question, such as whether you have enough or when the package was opened. When you resolve it, move it to one of the other two groups. Do not take a whole pantry inventory into a grocery aisle if only five lines will change what you buy.

## Test whether the inventory did its job

After shopping, look for two simple results. Did you avoid adding an item you already had? Did you buy an ingredient that made both meals possible? If the answer is unclear, keep the next inventory to one shelf and one meal. You can expand it later if the extra detail will affect what you buy. For a shared household, keep the date on the note and replace it when somebody uses the last of an item.

## Leave a note you can reuse next week

Write the date and cross off what you use for the two meals. Next week, start with the same short list if the ingredients are still relevant. If the system takes too long, limit it to the foods you buy twice by accident or run out of most often. The useful result is fewer guesses before shopping.
