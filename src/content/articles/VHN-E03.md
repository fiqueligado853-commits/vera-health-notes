The intake form asks what you take. Your pharmacy app has one list, the patient portal has another, and the bottle in your kitchen has the name of a supplement you bought yourself. Trying to reconcile the three in the waiting room is hard, especially if you also have to remember what changed at your last visit.

Use one page to record what you are actually using, what the labels or instructions say, and anything you want the healthcare team to verify. One page is a starting format. Add a second sheet if you need it. The goal is a list you can read aloud, hand over, or share through a channel your healthcare team uses.

## Start with what is in use now

Gather current containers and any written instructions you can easily find. Look for prescription medicines, over-the-counter products, and dietary supplements. Check for patches, creams, drops, or inhalers you keep outside the usual medicine cabinet. Include items you only use occasionally. If you take something less often than the package suggests or have not started something that still appears in the portal, write down the difference. Your notes describe what is happening. You can ask your healthcare team to reconcile the records.

The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements medication and supplement record](https://ods.od.nih.gov/pubs/DietarySupplementandMedicineRecord.pdf) gives you space for product names and ingredients, how much you use, how often you use it, and why you use it. Its fields are useful even if you keep your list in a phone note. Put the update date at the top. If you find another copy later, you will be able to tell which one you brought to the visit.

Scan the places you normally keep or receive products. If you refill prescriptions at more than one pharmacy, check both records. If you use a supplement that you bought without a prescription, it may not appear in either record. Add it by copying the name from the package. For products with long ingredient lists, photograph the whole panel and note that you have the image with you. Our [guide to reading supplement labels](/articles/supplement-label-serving-size-traps/) shows where to find the amount and serving size on those panels.

## Make each row easy to check

Use one row per product. A paper copy does not need a complicated form. The fields below help someone else understand what your notes mean. If you cannot fill one in confidently, add a question mark and bring whatever record or package you have.

| What to put on the list | What to write down |
| --- | --- |
| Product and strength | Full name and the amount shown on the container or prescription label |
| How you use it | Amount you actually use at a time, how often, and whether your use is occasional |
| Recent change | Approximate start, stop, or change date if relevant and known |
| Why or who | Why you use it or who prescribed it, if you know |
| Question for the visit | Any disagreement between the label, portal, and what you actually use |

Keep the strength on the label separate from the amount you use. A tablet's printed strength and the number of tablets taken are two details. Similarly, a supplement can show an amount per serving while your list records the number of capsules you actually take. Having both lines lets the healthcare team compare them without guessing what you meant.

For an occasional product, write roughly how you use it if you remember. For example, “used twice last week, date uncertain” is more useful in the right conversation than an empty box or a made-up schedule. That wording is an example of a record, not advice about how often to take anything. If your label and your written instructions differ, record both and ask the clinician or pharmacist to clarify which instructions apply.

## Flag two copies that disagree

Say your portal lists a prescription as current, while your paper discharge record says it changed. Put a short note beside that entry and bring the discharge record if you have it. If a clinician gave you different instructions and you do not remember when, write “instructions differ, date uncertain.” Clear notes about uncertainty give your healthcare team something specific to check.

If you have two bottles that look similar, copy the full names and strengths from both. Keep discontinued items in a short recent-changes note if you think they matter to the conversation. That leaves the main list easy to scan while giving the team a timeline. If you have no idea what an item is, bring the labeled container or a clear photo rather than identifying it by color or appearance.

Include a short note about suspected reactions or allergies you have already been asked to record, using wording you can explain at the visit. Keep that note separate from the list of products in use. Describe what you remember and when it happened. The purpose here is to bring information to the appointment. Your clinician can decide what it means for your care.

## Bring a version you can share

A printout is easy to hand over. A phone note can work just as well if you can open it quickly and read it. For a video visit, keep the list in reach and ask the office which secure channel it uses if it wants a copy. Bring the labeled containers or photos if there are discrepancies. The [AHRQ medication management patient flyer](https://www.ahrq.gov/sites/default/files/wysiwyg/professionals/quality-patient-safety/patient-family-engagement/pfeprimarycare/medication-mgmt-patient-guide.pdf) describes bringing medicines to an appointment so the care team can review what you use.

Put the questions you most want answered at the top. You could ask which items the team wants to update in the portal or whether it needs the full package photo for a supplement. If a family member is helping you prepare, read the list together and mark which details you can verify. Ask before sharing the list with anyone who does not need it for your care.

### If someone else helps keep track

When a family member or caregiver helps with the list, agree on who will keep the current version and how you will tell when it was updated. For instance, you might put the date on the printed page and keep the same dated copy on a phone. If one person picked up a prescription while another heard the instructions, add a note about where each detail came from. Do not fill a blank from memory if someone else can check the container.

You can also use the last few lines as a small handoff for the visit. Mark the items you want the team to confirm first. Leave space for the response or for a note telling you where the updated instructions were sent. Once you have an answer, date the revision so you can distinguish it from the copy you brought.

## Leave with one update to make

Before the visit ends, ask what the healthcare team wants changed on your list and what you should do if a pharmacy label or portal entry still differs. If you receive written instructions, save them with the dated list so they are easy to locate next time. Keeping one copy current is easier than rebuilding the whole list from memory at every appointment.

If you also bring home blood pressure records, our [free printable log](/resources/blood-pressure-log/) has space for those readings. Keep the log and the medication list as separate sheets so each is easy to scan. For the medication list itself, you can copy the table above or use the [NIH's three-page blank record](https://ods.od.nih.gov/pubs/DietarySupplementandMedicineRecord.pdf) if you want more room. You can start with the items you know and mark the rows you need help completing.
