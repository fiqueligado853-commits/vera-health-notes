Both bread bags say something about grain. One is brown and reads “multigrain.” The other labels a specific whole grain on the front. Look past the color and copy the first few ingredients from each bag before drawing a conclusion about what grain is in it.

## Read a grain name, not a color

The ingredient list is where you can look for the names of whole-grain ingredients, such as whole wheat or rolled oats. FDA’s [whole-grain label draft guidance](https://www.fda.gov/food/guidance-documents-regulatory-information-topic/draft-guidance-industry-and-fda-staff-whole-grain-label-statements) explains the role of bran, germ, and endosperm in the whole grain. It is a useful reference for terms, not a substitute for reading the actual package. A grain can contribute color without telling you whether all the original parts are present.

## Decide what “multigrain” leaves unanswered

The front of the bag may say that several grains are used. Your question is whether those grains are whole, refined, or a mix. A package can list several different grain ingredients and still leave their individual quantities unknown. When a package says “made with whole grain,” keep looking for what else is named. The front wording and the ingredient list have different jobs.

## Compare two illustrative labels

Imagine Loaf A starts with whole wheat flour, water, and rolled oats. Loaf B starts with enriched wheat flour, water, and whole rye. Both imaginary loaves name a whole-grain ingredient. A places whole wheat first and also names oats. B names whole rye after other ingredients. The label lets you describe that difference. It does not give you a reliable percentage to assign to each loaf from the sequence alone.

## Use dietary fiber as a second question

If you are comparing the fiber shown on the Nutrition Facts panels, put the loaves on a similar serving basis. Two slices from a wide loaf and two slices from a small loaf may have different weights. Record the fiber separately from the whole-grain check. A fiber number does not identify which grain or other ingredient supplied it.

## Build one useful photo pair

At the store, take one photo of the ingredient list and a second of the Nutrition Facts panel on the same bag. If the bag mentions a specific amount of whole grain, photograph that claim too. This makes it possible to check which statement was made and which version of the bread you saw. For a family shopper who only needs an answer for this week, the useful result can be as simple as two confirmed ingredient names. There is no need to score the whole shelf.

## Make the comparison easy to repeat

Write down the exact loaf name, the grain ingredients you identified, and the labeled slice weight if you are comparing a Nutrition Facts line. Use the current package instead of a picture from a previous order. Brands can sell more than one recipe with similar packaging. If the difference is still unclear, keep it as a question rather than filling it with the color of the bread or a picture of an oat stalk.
